How dogs learn

01

Rewards build behavior

Dogs repeat what pays. Food, play and praise after a good behavior grow that behavior; scolding after a bad one grows nothing but worry. Reward the calm, good choices — every time, in the moment.

02

Keep sessions tiny — and end on a win

Two to five minutes, a few times a day. Always finish on an easy success, then stop while it's fun. Five short games beat one long grind, every single time.

03

Mark the moment, then pay

Your marker word ('Yes!') pinpoints the exact instant they got it right, and the treat follows within one second. Late payment pays whatever they were doing when it arrived — timing beats volume.

04

One cue, once

Say 'sit' once, then help them succeed (move closer, guide). Repeating cues trains dogs to wait for the third or fourth call. If it failed, the distance or distraction was too big — not the word.

05

Proof in slices: the three D's

A skill is only 'known' where it was practiced. Grow Duration, Distance and Distraction one at a time — and drop the other two while adding a new one. Small rungs, quietly climbed.

06

Manage while learning

Gates, leads, closed doors and pick-ups aren't cheating: they stop your dog from rehearsing mistakes while training catches up. Management is what buys the weeks that training needs.

07

Follow the 3-3-3 clock

Shelter dogs use roughly 3 days to decompress, 3 weeks to learn routines, and 3 months to fully belong. And some dogs need longer — no comparing, no rushing. Decompression comes before training.

08

Food is currency, not bribery

Hand-feed and scatter-feed the first weeks (you become good news), pay pea-sized treats for work, and save the fancy stuff (cheese, chicken) for big moments. Treats stay under about 10% of daily food.

09

Read body language first

Lip licking, yawning, whale eye, tucked tails are polite 'no thanks' signals; growling and snapping are the last-resort system. See a stress tell? Lower the pressure and add space — that's behavior prevention.

10

Consent beats handling

Petting, grooming, vet care — everything works better when the dog can say 'not yet' (chin lifts = pause). Dogs who trust their 'no' are calmer, safer and easier to handle for life.

Body language first

Stress signals

  • Lip licking or tongue flicks (not eating)
  • Yawning or sniffing the ground (blinking out of context)
  • Whale eye — whites of the eyes showing
  • Ears pinned back or stiff high-held head
  • Tucked or frozen-low tail
  • Pacing, trembling or shakes
  • Panting without heat or exercise
  • Suddenly 'not hungry' or refusing treats
  • Hiding, escaping to another room
  • Stiff 'slow' wags or freeze

Relaxed signals

  • Loose, wiggly body and bouncy moves
  • Wide sweeping tail (not stiff or upright)
  • Soft eyes and easy blinking
  • Relaxed open mouth, easy breathing
  • Play bows and invitations
  • Takes treats readily
  • Solicits contact — leans in or nudges

The household signal rule

One stress signal = lower the pressure. Two together = stop what you're doing and give space. That's it — it's the whole trick.

Ten things to skip entirely

  • 🚫 Don't punish accidents or toilet mistakes — it teaches dogs to hide, not hold it.
  • 🐺 No alpha-rolls, dominance or 'pack owner' games — packs are families, not military. Force methods measurably increase fear and aggression (AVSAB).
  • 🏞️ No dog parks, crowds or pet stores in the first weeks — being flooded with scary things teaches fear, and fear grows barking and snapping later.
  • 🤲 No forced greetings or hugs — let your dog choose to approach, every time.
  • 🍲 Don't hover over food bowls or take bones away quietly — trade instead of robbing; guarding grows from grabby hands.
  • 🖐️ Don't grab a scared dog's collar — it makes collar-touch the enemy right when you need it at the vet.
  • 📞 Never call your dog to something bad — baths, punishments and park exits poison 'come'. Just go and collect them instead.
  • 📣 Don't repeat cues as an alarm — 'sit, SIT, SIT!' trains the third call to be the real one.
  • ⏰ Don't leave a new rescue home alone for long hours in week one — alone-time is built in steps, or separation panic grows instead.
  • 🐢 Don't compare timelines with anyone else's dog — shelter histories vary enormously; slower is usually the fastest route.

Your gear checklist

Everything you need to run the whole course fits in one shopping trip:

  • A well-fitted harness or flat collar (never prong, choke or shock)
  • A 1.8 m fixed lead — no retractables while training
  • A 5–10 m long line for sniff walks and recall proofing
  • Treat pouch + pea-sized soft treats
  • A food-stuffable toy (Kong-style) and safe chews
  • A mat or towel that can travel
  • Ex-pen or crate (optional, useful)
  • Enzyme cleaner for the toilet-training weeks

When to bring in a professional

  • Any snapping, lunging or biting incidents — call a certified behavior professional early, not 'if it gets worse'.
  • Fear that doesn't melt in ~2 weeks — still avoiding people or refusing food at home.
  • Separation panic: drool, scratches at doors, accidents within minutes of your absence.
  • Growing guarding posture around food, bones, beds or doorways (freeze, hard stare, lip curl, fast eating).
  • Sudden behavior changes — ask the vet first. Pain mimics almost every behavior problem.

Your first call: the adoption coordinator/behavior team at the shelter you adopted from. Then look for force-free credentials — CAAB or DACVB (behavior scientists), CPDT-KA (certified trainer), CSAT (separation anxiety). Fair question for any trainer: 'What exactly will you do when my dog gets it wrong?' Good answers never include pain or intimidation.
