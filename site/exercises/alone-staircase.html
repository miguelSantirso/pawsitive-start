Why this matters

Rescues are at higher risk for separation distress — losing a person is suspected to be a trigger — and prevention costs a fraction of treatment. The ASPCA plan behind this ladder: keep every absence shorter than their first worried moment, keep goodbyes boring, and let the long-lasting food toy be the sign 'you're leaving, I'll be fine'.

What you need

  • A gate or crate your dog accepts
  • A long-lasting stuffed food toy (freeze it)
  • A phone camera
  • Keys + coat

Mastery check

Common mistakes

  • Never 'test' past their tolerance to see what happens — every panic session digs the hole deeper.
  • Don't make departures dramatic; don't reward frantic hellos with big attention.
  • Don't scold barking or mess after you return — post-hoc punishments teach nothing but fear of your return.

If your dog is struggling

We're stuck; minutes never go past five without whining.

Camp at that rung for days — remove the departure cues (no coat ritual), longer chews, quieter returns. If true distress (drool, self-injury, accidents in minutes) shows up, call a certified behavior pro early: this usually needs professional help and rarely resolves on its own.

They only panic when we drive off.

Same ladder, different shape: pretend-drive rehearsal (sit in the car, engine on, drive 20 seconds, back); some dogs need the car as its own stage-one rung.

Source: ASPCA separation anxiety guide
